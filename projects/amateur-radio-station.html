Project

Amateur Radio Station Infrastructure

RF Systems, Antenna Installation, Grounding, Signal Integrity, and Mobile Communications

Operational / Ongoing Expansion

Short Description

A two-part amateur radio communications system consisting of a fixed home station and a concealed mobile installation. The project involved antenna selection, low-loss coaxial routing, lightning protection, grounding and bonding, RF interference troubleshooting, vehicle integration, and planning for future 10-meter capability.

Project Overview

My amateur radio setup consists of two primary systems: a home base station and a mobile station installed in my vehicle. Although both systems began with relatively simple communication goals, their installations required solving several practical problems involving radio-frequency performance, grounding, cable routing, interference, electrical safety, and physical integration.

The home station is built around a Diamond X510HD base antenna connected through approximately 35 feet of LMR-600 coaxial cable. The cable enters the attic, passes through the wall, and terminates near my desk through an SWR meter before connecting to a Baofeng AR-5RM handheld radio.

The current radio is modest compared with the antenna and feed-line infrastructure, but it is sufficient for my present needs and gives me a strong foundation for upgrading to a dedicated base transceiver later.

The mobile station uses a TYT TH-9800D Plus installed in my vehicle. I wanted the exterior installation to remain visually discreet, so I replaced the original FM antenna with a Diamond NR770HBNMO dual-band mobile antenna. This preserved a conventional antenna appearance while providing significantly better performance for amateur radio operation.

Home Base Station

Antenna and Feed Line

The home station uses a Diamond X510HD vertical base antenna with roughly 35 feet of LMR-600 coax. The low-loss coax was selected to reduce feed-line attenuation between the antenna and radio, particularly across the VHF and UHF frequencies used by the station.

The coax is routed from the exterior antenna into the attic, through the wall, and down to the operating position near my desk. An SWR meter is installed between the feed line and radio so the system can be monitored for antenna matching and abnormal behavior.

Lightning Protection and Grounding

Lightning and static discharge protection were major concerns during the installation. A Diamond SP3000 lightning arrestor was installed near the antenna and connected to a dedicated grounding electrode using 6 AWG grounding conductor.

A separate antenna ground without proper bonding to the building grounding system could create dangerous potential differences during severe static or lightning conditions. To avoid leaving the systems at different electrical potentials, the antenna grounding electrode was bonded into the building grounding system through the available foundation grounding connection.

Interference and Common-Mode Suppression

During testing, I encountered destructive interaction associated with the metal fascia near the antenna mount. The antenna assembly was electrically isolated from the fascia to reduce its unintended influence on the RF system.

I also suspected that RF energy was traveling along the outside of the coaxial shield. Ferrite suppression was added near the antenna feed point and again near the radio end of the coax to reduce common-mode current and help isolate the feed line from the radiating system.

Mobile Station

The vehicle installation is built around a TYT TH-9800D Plus mobile radio. The primary design goal was to create a functional installation without making the vehicle look heavily modified or drawing unnecessary attention to the radio equipment.

To accomplish this, I replaced the original vehicle FM antenna with a Diamond NR770HBNMO dual-band mobile antenna. Its physical form remains reasonably similar to the original antenna while providing an appropriate RF system for the mobile transceiver.

The original antenna mounting hardware required modification because the amateur radio antenna used a fundamentally different connection system. Coaxial cable and dedicated power wiring were then routed through the vehicle firewall to the radio installation.

The power system was designed to provide a strong electrical connection and reduce noise while also allowing the radio to be fully shut down so it could not slowly discharge the vehicle battery when parked.

Technical Challenges

  • Grounding and bonding: creating a low-impedance discharge path while ensuring the antenna grounding system was bonded to the building grounding system.
  • Lightning protection: integrating an external arrestor and grounding conductor into the antenna installation.
  • Feed-line routing: bringing large-diameter LMR-600 coax from the exterior antenna through the attic and wall to the operating position.
  • Structural interaction: identifying and reducing unwanted RF interaction with nearby metal fascia.
  • Common-mode current: adding ferrite suppression to reduce RF traveling along the outside of the coax shield.
  • Vehicle integration: adapting the original antenna location to accept an amateur radio mobile antenna.
  • Firewall routing: safely passing coaxial and power wiring between the engine compartment and vehicle interior.
  • Electrical noise: maintaining good bonding and power delivery while minimizing vehicle-generated interference.
  • Battery protection: ensuring the mobile radio could be completely shut down when not in use.
  • Discreet installation: preserving a relatively factory-like exterior appearance while adding a functional mobile radio system.

Future Expansion

I also have an MFJ-918 1.8–30 MHz balun that I plan to use as part of a future 10-meter antenna installation. The goal is to create a relatively subtle roof-mounted antenna that expands the home station's available frequency range without becoming visually intrusive.

A future upgrade will also replace the handheld currently used at the home operating position with a dedicated base or mobile-class transceiver that can better utilize the existing antenna and feed-line infrastructure.

Miscellaneous Notes

This project has been less about purchasing radio equipment and more about understanding how the entire RF installation behaves as a system. Antenna placement, nearby conductive structures, coaxial losses, shield current, grounding, bonding, and power routing all affect real-world performance.

The result is an operational home and mobile communications setup with a clear path for future upgrades. It has also provided practical experience with RF troubleshooting, vehicle electrical integration, lightning protection, and antenna infrastructure.
